WorldSlider: Generating Parallel Worlds of a Flight

Anonymous Authors
Paper arXiv Code Data

“What if you could slide into a thousand different worlds? … you’re the same person, but everything else is different?”

Quinn Mallory · Sliders

Consider a flight through a polar research station. The same flight can be revisited under a rose-colored sky, or transferred altogether to a tropical reservoir. The illumination, geometry, and identity of the scene change, yet the flight advances, banks, and turns with the same rhythm. We study the problem of generating these parallel worlds of a flight: visually distinct videos that all fly one prescribed trajectory.

Motivation

Limitations of parameter-conditioned camera control

Camera-conditioned video generation is well established, but existing methods are developed and evaluated predominantly on smooth, canonical trajectories. Under the compound motion of an executed flight — simultaneous translation, rotation, speed variation and altitude change — control degrades substantially. Below, Wan2.1-Fun-CC and RealCam-I2V are conditioned on the trajectory of the reference flight shown at left. The recovered camera motion departs from the prescribed path, and appearance degrades as the model reconciles a trajectory it cannot realize. We attribute this in part to the form of the conditioning signal: numeric camera parameters enter the generator as an auxiliary embedding rather than in the modality through which it represents scene motion. This motivates an explicitly visual control.

Abstract

We study the problem of generating parallel worlds of a flight — visually distinct videos that share the same flight trajectory — from pre-trained video generative models. Controlling such generation presents an inherent trade-off: camera parameters specify motion precisely but are foreign to video models, while visual references are easy to condition on but reveal the original world. To bridge this gap, we present WorldSlider, a general generative pipeline for synthesizing diverse visual worlds along prescribed flight trajectories. Central to our pipeline is a novel appearance-free visual control signal, constructed automatically from metric camera poses, that encodes both the trajectory and its time-varying free-space corridor in a form native to video generative models. To make generation efficient and further improve its fidelity to the prescribed trajectory, we augment few-step distillation with reward learning from a geometry-based camera-motion verifier. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework by generating a diverse range of parallel worlds for simulated flights, show its advantages over existing methods in trajectory preservation, and demonstrate cross-domain flight transfer and trajectory-conditioned branching from a shared observation.

Method

FlightGrid: an appearance-free flight representation

A flight must be specified to the generator without transferring the world in which it was recorded. Camera parameters specify motion exactly but are foreign to video models; visual references condition densely and naturally, yet necessarily disclose source appearance and geometry. FlightGrid resolves this trade-off as an appearance-free visual control signal constructed automatically from metric camera poses. It comprises a time-indexed flight trace, which preserves the temporal parameterization of the trajectory, and a metric spatial scaffold, which supplies a persistent world reference. The rendered signal contains no RGB pixels, scene depth, object boundaries or semantic labels from the source world.

Training proceeds in two stages. Control adaptation injects FlightGrid, the first frame and the text condition into a pre-trained video DiT through a VACE-style control branch; the backbone, the VAE and the text encoder remain frozen, so optimization is restricted to the control branch under the conditional rectified-flow objective. The resulting multi-step teacher is then distilled into a few-step student by score-based distribution matching. Following a standard DMD warm-up, a geometry-based verifier recovers the camera trajectory of each student sample and scores its agreement with the prescribed flight; that score, calibrated on the source flight before any generated sample is evaluated, reweights the sample’s own DMD update. Distillation thus reduces sampling cost and improves trajectory fidelity under an unchanged teacher-matching objective.

Ablations

Ablation of the principal components

The two principal components are ablated independently. For the flight representation, the time-indexed trace (−T), the route-side vertical markers (−M), or the adaptive intermediate layers (−L) are removed while the horizontal grids are retained, and the 35-step adaptation stage is retrained; −ML removes the markers and the layers together. Every removal increases trajectory error relative to the complete representation, the trace contributing most, and the penalty is consistently larger on diverse flights than on standard trajectories — consistent with the control being relied upon where source appearance is a weak predictor of subsequent motion. For distillation, the verifier is held fixed while the reward temperature is varied, and the temperature is then fixed while verifier capacity is varied; the resulting models are scored with an independent estimator so that the reward is not evaluated against itself. All temperatures exceed the unweighted DMD baseline at convergence, and trajectory error decreases monotonically with verifier capacity.

Trajectory-conditioned branching from a shared observation

The construction inverts. Both branches below are conditioned on a single observation — the frame at left — and on two FlightGrids that diverge in heading. Each branch synthesizes a world consistent with that shared observation while revealing only the region its own trajectory traverses.

Comparison

Comparison with camera-controlled video generation

All methods are evaluated under a common protocol, with every output rescored by the same ViPE and Q-Align pipeline. On standard trajectories, translation error is comparable across the strongest methods — 0.097 for WorldSlider against 0.099 for Wan2.1-Fun — while rotation error already separates, 2.42° against 5.47°. On the held-out diverse flights the methods separate decisively: every baseline falls between 24° and 27° mean geodesic rotation error, whereas WorldSlider attains 6.65° at four denoising function evaluations rather than 25 or 50.

Discussion

Downstream application: visual robustness for flight policies

Because a parallel world preserves the trajectory that generated it, the action labels of the source flight remain valid in the synthesized video. A single recorded flight therefore yields a family of visually distinct training examples, under illumination, geometry and scene identity the vehicle has never encountered. We intend to pursue this as the principal downstream application of the framework: visual robustness for flight control policies, and more broadly for embodied agents, for which deployment in an unfamiliar environment should constitute a change of appearance rather than a distribution shift.
